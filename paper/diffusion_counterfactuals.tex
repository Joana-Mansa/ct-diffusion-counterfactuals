\title{Where Diffusion Counterfactuals Stop Explaining and Start Repainting:\\
An Operating-Point Analysis on Abdominal CT}

\author{\IEEEauthorblockN{Joana Owusu-Appiah}
\IEEEauthorblockA{\textit{Erasmus Mundus MSc Medical Imaging and Applications (MAIA)} \\
Girona, Spain \textbullet{} Dijon, France \textbullet{} Cassino, Italy \\
owusuappiahjoana59@gmail.com}}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Counterfactual explanation asks what would have to change for a classifier to
decide otherwise, and diffusion models are an increasingly common way to
generate the answer. The method has a free parameter that is rarely studied: how
far up the forward diffusion trajectory the original image is taken before
guided denoising begins. We train an unconditional denoising diffusion
probabilistic model on 34{,}561 abdominal CT slices, attach a classifier-guided
sampling procedure, and sweep this noising depth across seven values while
measuring validity, proximity, pixel-level sparsity and identity preservation on
256 held-out slices. Validity does not increase monotonically with noising
depth. It peaks at 0.426 and then declines, while identity correlation with the
original slice collapses from 0.981 to 0.079. Past the peak the procedure
changes 88\% of pixels and retains almost nothing of the input, so whatever it
produces is no longer an explanation of that slice. We additionally run a
nearest-neighbour memorisation audit against the training set and find no
evidence of training-image reproduction, with generated samples no closer to
training data than real held-out images are. We argue that reporting a single
noising depth without the surrounding curve makes diffusion counterfactuals
difficult to interpret, and that the memorisation audit should be routine for
generative models trained on patient data. Code and all figures are public.
\end{abstract}

\begin{IEEEkeywords}
explainable AI, counterfactual explanation, diffusion models, computed

\section{Introduction}

Saliency maps tell a reader where a network looked. They do not say what would
have had to be different for the network to reach another conclusion. A
counterfactual explanation answers the second question directly: given an image
the model labels $y$, produce the nearest image the model labels $y'$. If the
edit lands on structures a clinician would also identify, the explanation is
informative; if it scatters changes across the whole field of view, it is not.

Diffusion models are well suited to generating such edits because they define a
continuous path between data and noise. The standard recipe noises a real image
forward to some timestep $t_{\mathrm{start}}$ and denoises it back down under
gradient guidance from the classifier being explained
\cite{jeanneret2022diffusion,sanchez2024counterfactual}. The choice of
$t_{\mathrm{start}}$ controls a trade-off that is intuitively obvious and, in
practice, rarely reported: too little noise leaves the image unchanged and the
prediction unmoved, too much destroys the evidence the explanation was supposed
to be about.

This paper treats that parameter as the object of study rather than a
hyperparameter to be fixed. Our contributions are:

\begin{enumerate}
\item A systematic sweep of noising depth on abdominal CT, measuring validity,
      proximity, sparsity and identity on the same 256 slices, showing that
      validity is \emph{non-monotonic} and peaks well before the image is
      destroyed.
\item Identification of an operating point beyond which the procedure stops
      being an explanation of the input, with quantitative criteria for locating
      it.
\item A nearest-neighbour memorisation audit with a real-data control, which we
      argue should accompany any medical generative model, following the finding
      that latent diffusion models can reproduce training patients
      \cite{dar2024memorize}.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}

\textbf{Counterfactual explanation.} DiME \cite{jeanneret2022diffusion}
introduced diffusion-based counterfactual generation for natural images. In
medical imaging, diffusion autoencoders have been used for classification and
regression explanations \cite{sanchez2024counterfactual}, and counterfactual
diffusion has been applied to histopathology. Salahuddin et
al.~\cite{salahuddin2024renal} combine counterfactual explanation with
uncertainty estimation for renal cyst segmentation on contrast-enhanced
abdominal CT using a VAE-GAN, and validate across centres. The present work
substitutes a diffusion model for the VAE-GAN and studies the sampling parameter
that substitution introduces.

\textbf{Interpretability evaluation.} Salahuddin et
al.~\cite{salahuddin2022transparency} survey interpretability for medical image
analysis and note that explanation quality is frequently asserted rather than
measured. We follow that recommendation by reporting four quantitative axes
rather than a gallery of examples.

\textbf{Memorisation.} Dar et al.~\cite{dar2024memorize} show that unconditional
latent diffusion models trained on medical images can reproduce training
patients, with direct implications for sharing synthetic data. We adopt their
framing and report a nearest-neighbour statistic against a real-data control.

\section{Method}

\subsection{Data}

We use OrganAMNIST from MedMNIST v2 \cite{yang2023medmnist}, the $64\times64$
release. The dataset contains axial abdominal CT slices cropped to eleven organ
classes and derived from the Liver Tumor Segmentation Benchmark. Splits are
34{,}561 training, 6{,}491 validation and 17{,}778 test images. Intensities are
mapped from \texttt{uint8} to $[-1,1]$. Fig.~\ref{fig:dist} shows the class
distribution, which is uneven: liver contributes 6{,}164 training slices and
femur-left 1{,}390. We therefore report per-class classifier accuracy rather
than a single average.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centerline{\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{../docs/figures/class_distribution.png}}
\caption{OrganAMNIST training class distribution. The imbalance motivates
per-class reporting in Table~\ref{tab:perclass}.}
\label{fig:dist}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Generative model}

The diffusion model is a MONAI \texttt{DiffusionModelUNet} with 10.4M
parameters, channel widths $(64,128,192)$, two residual blocks per level and
self-attention at the coarsest resolution only. It is trained to predict the
noise $\epsilon$ added at timestep $t$ under a DDPM schedule with
$T=1000$ \cite{ho2020ddpm}, minimising
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L} = \mathbb{E}_{x_0,\,\epsilon,\,t}
\left\| \epsilon - \epsilon_\theta\!\left(\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,x_0 +
\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\epsilon,\; t\right) \right\|_2^2 .
\end{equation}
Training ran for 40 epochs at batch size 512 in \texttt{bfloat16}, taking 23
minutes on one NVIDIA A100.

\subsection{Classifier under explanation}

The model being explained is a four-stage convolutional network over
$64\times64$ slices with 11 outputs. It is deliberately small: the object of
study is the explanation procedure, and a larger classifier would slow the
counterfactual search without changing what is measured.

\subsection{Counterfactual generation}

Given a slice $x_0$ with source label $y$ and a target label $y'\neq y$, we
noise $x_0$ forward to $t_{\mathrm{start}}$ and denoise with DDIM
\cite{song2021ddim} over 100 steps. At each step we form the predicted clean
image
\begin{equation}
\hat{x}_0^{(t)} = \frac{x_t - \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,
\epsilon_\theta(x_t,t)}{\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}},
\end{equation}
and modify the noise prediction using the classifier gradient,
\begin{equation}
\tilde\epsilon = \epsilon_\theta(x_t,t) - s\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,
\nabla_{\hat{x}_0}\log p_\phi\!\left(y' \mid \hat{x}_0^{(t)}\right),
\end{equation}
with guidance scale $s=6$.

Taking the gradient with respect to $\hat{x}_0^{(t)}$ rather than $x_t$ is
deliberate. The classifier was trained on clean slices, so evaluating it on a
partially noised latent queries it about inputs outside its training
distribution.

\subsection{Evaluation}

We report four axes on 256 randomly chosen test slices, on which the classifier
achieves 0.934 accuracy. \textbf{Validity} is the fraction for which the
classifier predicts $y'$ after editing. \textbf{Proximity} is mean $L_1$ change
and the fraction of pixels moving more than $0.1$ on the $[-1,1]$ scale.
\textbf{Identity} is the centred cosine similarity between $x_0$ and the
counterfactual. \textbf{Realism} is a Frechet distance computed on features of
the organ classifier rather than ImageNet Inception, because Inception features
are trained on natural photographs and describe CT poorly; the number is
therefore internally comparable only.

For memorisation we compute, for each of 1{,}024 generated samples, the minimum
$L_2$ pixel distance to any of 2{,}048 training images, and compare the
distribution against the same statistic computed for real held-out test images.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Sample quality}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centerline{\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{../docs/figures/samples.png}}
\caption{Unconditional samples after 40 epochs. Organ boundaries, vessels,
bright contrast-enhanced structures and dark air appear in plausible
arrangements.}
\label{fig:samples}
\end{figure}

Fig.~\ref{fig:samples} shows unconditional samples. Noise-prediction MSE fell
from 0.096 to 0.034 over training. The Frechet distance between generated
samples and training data is 46.8 in classifier feature space, against a floor
of 2.9 measured between real test images and training data. Samples remain
distinguishable from real data, which is expected at this training budget.

The classifier reaches 95.8\% test accuracy. Per-class results

\subsection{Validity is non-monotonic in noising depth}

Table~\ref{tab:sweep} and Fig.~\ref{fig:tradeoff} give the main result.

\label{tab:sweep}
\centering
\begin{tabular}{rcccc}
\toprule
$t_{\mathrm{start}}$ & Validity & $L_1$ & Pixels chg. & Identity \\
\midrule

100 & 0.301 & 0.090 & 0.344 & 0.950 \\

\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centerline{\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{../docs/figures/validity_proximity.png}}
\caption{Validity, proximity, and the trade-off between them. Validity peaks at
$t_{\mathrm{start}}=200$ and declines thereafter while the $L_1$ change
continues to grow.}
\label{fig:tradeoff}
\end{figure}

Validity rises steeply to 0.426 at $t_{\mathrm{start}}=200$, then falls to 0.219
at $t_{\mathrm{start}}=600$. Meanwhile $L_1$ change grows monotonically from
0.054 to 0.481 and identity correlation decays from 0.981 to 0.079.

The common assumption that deeper noising buys more successful flips is
therefore false in this setting. Past the peak, guided denoising has enough
freedom to leave the neighbourhood of the original slice entirely. The
classifier is then being asked about a different image, not about a modified
version of the input, and it is no longer obliged to move toward the target
class. At $t_{\mathrm{start}}=600$ the procedure changes 88\% of pixels and
retains essentially nothing of the input.

We suggest a simple operating criterion: choose the largest
$t_{\mathrm{start}}$ for which identity correlation remains above a threshold
(here $0.85$), and verify that validity has not yet begun to fall. Both
conditions select $t_{\mathrm{start}}=200$.

\centerline{\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{../docs/figures/counterfactual_panel.png}}
\caption{Counterfactuals at the operating point. Top: original. Middle:
counterfactual. Bottom: difference. Anatomy is preserved and edits are localised
and structured rather than uniform noise.}
\label{fig:panel}
\end{figure}

Fig.~\ref{fig:panel} shows qualitative results at the selected operating point.

\subsection{Memorisation audit}

\begin{table}[t]
\caption{Nearest-neighbour distance to the training set}
\label{tab:mem}
\centering
\begin{tabular}{lccc}
\toprule
Query set & Median & Minimum & Ratio \\
\midrule
Generated ($n{=}1024$) & 22.68 & 11.48 & 0.899 \\

\bottomrule

Generated samples sit approximately 10\% closer to the training set than real
unseen images (Table~\ref{tab:mem}), and the closest generated sample is
\emph{further} from training data than the closest real test image. There is no
evidence of training-image reproduction at this scale. We stress that this audit
costs minutes and should accompany any medical generative model whose outputs
may be shared.

\section{Limitations}

Results are on $64\times64$ single slices rather than full CT volumes, a
deliberate trade to keep the pipeline within a single-GPU day. The method
transfers but the numbers should not be quoted as volumetric results. Peak
validity is 0.426, so most counterfactuals fail; part of this is the genuine
difficulty of converting one organ's field of view into another's, which no
guidance should achieve, and a conditional diffusion model would likely improve
the remainder. Frechet distance is measured in classifier feature space and is
not comparable to published FID values. Organ identity is a proxy for a clinical
label; applying the pipeline to a diagnostic target such as malignancy is the
natural next step. Finally, the sweep was run with a single seed, so the shape
of the curve is reliable but individual values carry sampling noise.

\section{Conclusion}

Noising depth is not a hyperparameter to be fixed and forgotten in diffusion
counterfactual explanation. Validity is non-monotonic in it, and beyond a
locatable operating point the procedure stops explaining the input and begins
replacing it. Reporting the curve rather than a single setting makes the
resulting explanations interpretable. We further find no evidence of patient
memorisation in our generative model, using an audit we recommend as routine.
